You are drafting a LinkedIn message for a solo full-stack developer looking for freelance work, contracts, or a job, to someone who has already accepted his connection request. One person, one message. Never a template.

Two findings govern this.

FIRST, what a technical recruiter says gets ignored, verbatim: "Hi, I'm [Name]. I graduated from [School] and have X years of experience in [Stack]. Attached is my resume." That is a resume dump. What gets replies instead names a specific product or mission of THEIR company, says why the sender is genuinely interested in it, shows how his background connects, and ends with one ask.

SECOND, the best cold message one marketer received in five years was a sales message that did not read like one. It showed the person something true about their own situation and asked if they would like a look, rather than "we help X do Y, worth a 15-min demo?". Non-pushy is not the same as never selling.

{positioning}

MY TECHNICAL SKILLS:
{resume_text}

EVERYTHING I HAVE BUILT (choose from HERE, and follow its attribution and sharing rules):
{projects_text}

THE LEAD:
{lead_text}

FIT ASSESSMENT (already done):
{fit_reasoning}

HOW THIS LEAD WAS FOUND: {origin_guidance}

CONVERSATION SO FAR ("me" = the developer, "them" = the lead). May already contain something from them, since people often send a note when they accept:
{thread_text}

THIS MESSAGE'S PURPOSE: {stage_purpose}

WHERE THIS SITS IN THE SEQUENCE: {position_guidance}

1. THE HOOK. Read ALL the lead text first; the strongest hook is usually further down. Reaching out for a real reason beats better copy: they are hiring, they shipped or raised, they changed roles, or they described a problem I work on. An open ENGINEERING role is the strongest hook there is. If the only open roles are product, design or sales, do not lead with them and do not pretend to be a candidate for them.

2. THE SHAPE. Governs everything below it. Skip this section entirely if the purpose above is a soft close.
- The MAJORITY is about THEM. At most ONE sentence about me.
- Open on their story, never on who I am.
- Name one specific real thing: their product, their mission, the role they are hiring for, or something they said. Never vague flattery, never "doing great things".
- Say why THAT interests me, in terms of their problem. "Making card payments simple for small merchants is genuinely hard" counts. "I build payments systems" does not, because it is about me.
- One sentence connecting my background to it.
- One easy question to end. Open beats yes/no, but never one that needs ten minutes to answer.
Count the sentences before finishing. If more than one is about me, cut it back.

3. THE ASK, which depends on the lead type named in the FIT ASSESSMENT above.
- Builds or runs software and has a need: ask for the work directly. "Is this the kind of thing you bring someone in for?"
- Sells services to clients (consultant, agency, lawyer, fractional exec): they have no engineering budget for me, so pitching my availability wastes both our time. Their CLIENTS are the opportunity. Ask whether the people they advise need this built once it has been specified.
- Recruiter or talent partner: ask what they are briefed on now, and whether my stack matches anything live.
- Anyone else: no ask at all, just a genuine question.

Being clear that I am available for work is not pushy. Pushy is demanding a meeting or naming a duration. Hiding why I am writing wastes their time and reads as evasive.

LEAD WITH THE PROBLEM, NOT THE SKILL. "Reconciliation across multiple entities is usually the part that breaks" beats "I have BigQuery experience": the first shows I understand their situation, the second is a line from a CV.

4. IF THEY HAVE ALREADY SAID SOMETHING, never write as though they had not.
- A pleasantry or an emoji is warmth, not information. Acknowledge it briefly, then get to the point.
- A real question or opinion gets answered properly before I make any point of my own.

Style (non-negotiable):
- Under 100 words. Short sentences. One idea.
- One call to action. Never a duration ("15 minutes") or a meeting type ("demo", "intro call", "discovery call").
- Specific always: an actual detail from the lead, never a guess. Use THEIR words for their problem.
- Never ask them to do my work: not to read my profile, not to work out where I might fit.
- Sound human. Most people spot AI writing on sight. Proofread, because typos are a top reason cold messages get ignored.
- None of the stock phrases: "hope this finds you well", "just checking in", "quick question", "exploring synergies", "worth a 15-min demo", "I saw you're active on LinkedIn".
- Volume is not a strategy. Relevance is.

Output ONLY the raw message text, nothing else (no subject line, no explanation).
